Campaign archive

Ads I keep thinking about.

Three campaigns I return to, analysed the way I'd actually talk about them: the idea, the consumer psychology, what the brand got right, and where I'd push back.

01 Visual: add a still or video thumbnail
Orphea Ambient OOH Milan, 2013

The Billboard Trap

An ad that did the killing. Orphea turned a Milan billboard into a working insect trap and let real insects draw the proof.

In spring 2013, Orphea turned a Milan billboard into a working insect trap and used thousands of real insects to dramatise its product promise. It is a sharp example of show, don't tell in outdoor advertising.

The brief was familiar: convince people that an insecticide works. Most brands would show a can, a dead fly and a claim. Publicis Italy did something stranger. The billboard showed a can of Orphea4D Protection on one side. On the area where the spray would come out, the agency applied a transparent adhesive in the shape of an aerosol jet.

The billboard was mounted at a busy Milan intersection, and night-time lighting drew insects toward it. At first, the spray was invisible. Over the following days, flies, mosquitoes and other bugs stuck to the adhesive, and the spray slowly appeared, drawn entirely in real insects. The ad didn't describe the promise. It performed it. Strictly, the adhesive did the trapping, not the insecticide, and that distinction matters. The critique below returns to it.

Ambient and guerrilla aren't mutually exclusive. Ambient marketing transforms an everyday environment or placement to carry the message, and here the billboard itself became the message. Guerrilla is the broader, unconventional, attention-grabbing approach it sits within. It isn't primarily experiential: people didn't interact with the billboard, they watched it change. The stunt element comes from the reveal over time and the film that spread it online.

Psychology: seeing is believing

The campaign leans on a long-standing idea in persuasion: visible evidence often lands harder than a verbal claim. A line like "kills insects fast" invites skepticism. Thousands of real, trapped insects don't argue. They just sit there, looking like proof.

It also uses curiosity and anticipation. A billboard that changes every day gives passers-by a reason to look again, which is rare for outdoor media that people usually tune out. Coverage at the time noted that the slowly filling billboard drew the curiosity of people in the area.

There is a visceral layer too. Insects trigger mild disgust in most people. The billboard makes that disgust visible on a giant scale, which reminds viewers of the problem the product solves, and makes the solution feel urgent.

Finally, the ad creates a wordless association between brand and action. Viewers don't have to read or decode anything. Can plus trapped bugs equals effective spray, and that link forms almost automatically.

Storytelling: a story told in days, not seconds

Most ads tell a story in 30 seconds or a single frame. The Billboard Trap tells its story over days. The narrative has a clear arc: an ordinary billboard, a mystery as dots begin to appear, and a reveal as the spray takes shape in insects.

The insects themselves are the storytellers. There's no copy doing the persuading, no actor, no voiceover on the billboard. The product's promise is dramatised literally, which makes the idea instantly understandable across languages.

The time-lapse case film then compresses the story back into a shareable format. Days of slow change become a satisfying minute of transformation, which is exactly the kind of content people pass around online.

Positioning: efficacy you can see

Insecticide is a crowded, low-interest category where most products look and sound the same. Orphea's positioning here is specific: the solution for outdoor insect control, proven in the open air. Instead of competing on claims, it competed on a demonstration.

The billboard also fits the product's outdoor-use promise. Placing the demonstration outside, in a real urban environment full of real insects, makes the message more credible than a lab-style claim in a TV spot.

It positions Orphea as clever and confident, a brand willing to put its promise in public and let the results speak. For a brand in a commodity category, that boldness is a point of difference in itself.

Cultural relevance: timing and a universal annoyance

The campaign ran in spring, as Milan's insect season began. That timing put the message in front of people exactly when flies and mosquitoes were becoming a daily irritation, so the problem felt immediate, not hypothetical.

The insight is also universal. Almost everyone, anywhere, finds bugs annoying, which is why a billboard in one Italian city travelled across advertising blogs and creative sites worldwide.

It also arrived at a moment when brands were discovering the power of turning physical media into shareable online content. The Billboard Trap belongs to a wave of early 2010s outdoor ideas built as much for the case film and the internet as for the street.

Execution: a billboard that worked the night shift

The execution was low-tech and precise. A standard billboard printed with the Orphea can, a transparent adhesive applied in the shape of the spray, and placement at a busy intersection where night-time lighting attracted insects. Fosbury ADV, the outdoor advertising concessionaire, supplied the space.

The real amplifier was the time-lapse case film. Publicis documented the billboard filling up over days and shared the video online, where it spread across creative and advertising sites. The street billboard reached one city. The film reached the world.

Reported results

The sell-in figure needs care. It covers the first quarter of 2013, while trade coverage of the billboard appeared in May, so the published sources don't show how much of that growth the billboard itself drove.

A fair critique

The biggest objection is also the simplest: the billboard proves that an adhesive traps insects, not that the spray kills them. What it demonstrated was that lit-up billboards attract insects, the surface could hold them, and the accumulating bodies could redraw the spray. A 2025 retrospective in The Drum says the adhesive contained Orphea's active ingredient, but contemporary 2013 reporting describes only an adhesive surface, so that claim is hard to confirm.

That gap makes the campaign a useful case study in the difference between demonstrating a product's effectiveness and creating the perception of it. The Billboard Trap does the second brilliantly.

There is also an environmental question. A trap that catches everything also catches harmless and beneficial insects, and audiences today are far more alert to pollinator decline than they were in 2013. The same idea might draw more pushback if launched now.

Neither point undoes the campaign's achievement. As a piece of communication, it made efficacy impossible to ignore, and it did so in a category most people never think about.

Why it works: the mechanism

  • Show, don't tell. The product benefit becomes something people can physically observe, so the persuasion happens without a single line of copy.
  • Media as the message. The billboard doesn't just carry the idea, it performs it. The medium and the claim are the same object.
  • Earned media. An unusual physical execution becomes a compelling time-lapse video, which spreads far beyond one street in Milan.

The takeaway

Orphea turned an ordinary billboard into a live product demonstration, letting real insects write the ad. In a crowded, low-interest category, the strongest claim is often the one you don't have to say out loud.

02 Visual: add a still or video thumbnail
IKEA Canada Experiential Canada, 2017

Cook This Page

A recipe you can bake. IKEA printed illustrated, measured recipe sheets onto parchment paper, so the instructions became the cooking tool.

IKEA Canada turned the recipe itself into a cooking tool: illustrated instructions printed on parchment paper that you fill with ingredients, roll up and bake. Cook This Page went on to win at Cannes Lions, the Clios, The One Show and D&AD in 2017.

The insight was simple and relatable. According to Leo Burnett Toronto, the agency behind it, most people find new foods and recipes intimidating. IKEA's job was to show that getting creative in the kitchen could be deliciously simple.

The answer borrowed from IKEA's most famous format, the wordless assembly diagram. Each recipe was printed in food-safe ink on oven-ready parchment, with illustrated outlines showing exactly where each ingredient goes. The outlines were drawn to size, so the drawings doubled as measurements. Fill in the blanks, roll the paper, put it in the oven, eat.

Each sealed recipe book held four recipes, including salmon with lemon and dill, a shrimp dish and a rhubarb raspberry crumble, and each recipe worked in IKEA food items. A total of 12,500 books were distributed across all 18 IKEA stores in Canada during an IKEA kitchen event in February 2017.

Utility-led marketing, direct marketing and earned media
Objective (primary)
Brand positioning: IKEA as a brand that simplifies everyday life
Objective (secondary)
Product discovery and promotion of IKEA's food range and kitchen event

The experience had two parts: discovering and collecting the recipe books in store, then actually cooking with them at home. That makes experiential a defensible primary label, and Applied Arts recognised the work in its Experiential/Event category. Guerrilla is a weak fit, since nothing here is a surprise intervention in public space. The award record reflects how hard the work is to box in: it won across Outdoor, Design, Promo & Activation, Direct and Craft categories.

Psychology: removing the fear from cooking

The campaign targets a real barrier: cooking anxiety. Recipes ask people to measure, interpret and sequence steps, and every one of those is a chance to feel like you're getting it wrong. Cook This Page removes most of those decisions. The drawing tells you where things go and roughly how much to use.

That reduces cognitive load, which is a well-established lever in behaviour design. When a task feels easier, people are more willing to try it. Turning a recipe into a fill-in-the-blank exercise lowers the effort needed to start.

It also creates a small sense of achievement. People follow the page, bake it and end up with a real meal they made themselves. The brand gets associated with that feeling of competence, which is a more lasting link than a discount.

There's a reciprocity effect too. IKEA gave people something genuinely useful and free, rather than just an ad. Being given something of value tends to create goodwill toward the giver.

These are explanations of why the idea could work. IKEA hasn't published research showing the campaign reduced cooking anxiety.

Storytelling: the recipe as an IKEA manual

The storytelling is almost entirely visual. Instead of a narrative ad, the brand tells its story through the object itself, in a format people already associate with IKEA: friendly illustrations, minimal words, step-by-step simplicity.

That is the clever part. IKEA's assembly instructions are part of popular culture, often the butt of jokes. Here, the brand takes that recognisable style and applies it to food, turning a familiar visual language into a delightful surprise.

The story then continues in the user's own kitchen. Each person who cooks a page creates their own little before-and-after moment, from a blank illustrated sheet to a finished dish, which is exactly the kind of transformation that photographs and spreads well online.

This is a clear case of brand asset transfer. IKEA has a recognisable visual language: minimal illustrations, step-by-step diagrams, almost no text. Instead of inventing a new style, it moved that existing identity into a new category, so the page communicated the brand without the logo doing all the work.

Positioning: from kitchen furniture to kitchen life

IKEA sells kitchens, cookware and food, but the campaign doesn't sell any of them directly. It sells the idea behind them: IKEA makes everyday life at home easier. Cook This Page extends that promise from building furniture to cooking dinner.

It also reinforces IKEA's democratic design positioning, good design made accessible to many people. A beautifully illustrated recipe that anyone can follow is that philosophy in miniature.

And it gives IKEA food a quiet role. Each recipe used IKEA food items, which nudges people to see the brand's food range as part of their own cooking, not just something eaten in the store cafeteria.

Cultural relevance: home cooking and shareable design

As a broader observation, food content was already highly visual and social by 2017, and Cook This Page was inherently photogenic: a blank illustrated sheet, then a finished dish. That made it easy for publications and audiences to share.

It also tapped into a widespread, very human feeling: wanting to cook more but feeling unsure how. That made the idea relevant far beyond Canada, which is why food and design publications around the world covered it.

The IKEA manual reference adds another cultural layer. Because so many people have struggled with flat-pack instructions, seeing that style turned into something easy and delicious carried a built-in wink.

Execution: craft is the campaign

The execution lives or dies on print craft. Recipes were printed with FDA-approved edible ink on parchment paper, and each four-recipe book was sealed to prevent contamination. Ingredient outlines were measured to size, so the illustration also worked as a portion guide, helping people judge how much sauce, salt or chive to add.

Distribution happened in two layers. Before the books reached shoppers, they were mailed to IKEA store managers along with a pan, so managers could try the recipes themselves and then set up the in-store event. The books were then made available in the food section of IKEA stores during the kitchen event. That internal step turned staff into advocates before the public ever saw the pages.

The bigger reach came from earned media. Food, design and lifestyle publications worldwide covered the idea, largely in 2017 as the award season began.

A fair critique

The campaign's footprint was small. Only 12,500 recipe books existed, and they appeared in one country during one event. Most people who know Cook This Page saw it online, not in their own kitchen.

Parchment cooking, or en papillote, isn't new either. IKEA's contribution was the design layer: printing a measured, illustrated recipe onto the paper. That's a genuine innovation in format, not in cooking technique.

And like many award-show favourites, its strength is clearer in craft and fame than in commercial results. It's a superb piece of brand building, but the public record doesn't show whether it sold more kitchens.

Why it works: the mechanism

  • Utility as advertising. The campaign gives people something genuinely useful, so the brand earns attention instead of buying it.
  • Brand asset transfer. It takes a format people instantly link to IKEA, the illustrated manual, and applies it to a new experience, so the idea feels both surprising and unmistakably IKEA.
  • Product-led brand positioning. Instead of claiming IKEA makes life simpler, it demonstrates it through something people can actually use.
  • Friction removal. Turning a recipe into fill-in-the-blanks lowers the effort barrier, so trying it feels easy.

The takeaway

Cook This Page shows that a brand can say "we make life at home easier" most convincingly by actually making something easier. The product of the campaign was the proof of the promise.

03 Visual: add a still or video thumbnail
NESCAFÉ Gold Film UK/Europe, 2017

For the Moments That Matter

"Those Few People." A 90-second film that turns a statistic about the people we meet into a feeling about the few who matter.

In October 2017, NESCAFÉ Gold relaunched in the UK, and then across Europe, with a 90-second film that whittles a crowd of everyone a man has ever met down to the handful who truly know him. The spot, "Those Few People," introduced the brand line "NESCAFÉ Gold. For the Moments That Matter."

The premise rests on a striking number. According to the brand, each of us meets around 80,000 people in a lifetime, but only a few become really special to us. The film turns that statistic into a scene.

A man stands on stage in front of a packed amphitheatre filled with the people he's met: university friends, people from nights out, co-workers. He asks people to sit down if they don't know his name, his childhood nickname, the one who got away, or if they've never seen him cry. Row by row, the crowd sits. By the end, only a few people remain standing. He asks if they have a moment now, and they share a coffee together.

One clarification matters for this analysis: despite often being described as a social experiment, it is a scripted, directed film, not a documented experiment with real acquaintances. It was shot in the ancient Roman theatre in Plovdiv, Bulgaria. The experiment is a storytelling device.

The campaign at a glance

Format (primary)
Emotional storytelling (TV, cinema and online film)
Format (secondary)
Integrated relaunch campaign: product TV spots, redesigned packaging, in-store decoration and PR
Objective (primary)
Brand positioning: NESCAFÉ Gold as the coffee for meaningful moments and connections (emotional branding)
Objective (secondary)
Product launch (relaunch of an improved recipe and redesigned jar) and a push into the premium instant coffee segment
Market
UK in October 2017, then across Europe; extended to Australia and New Zealand in 2018 through a locally developed campaign
Agency
Publicis Worldwide (Publicis London and Publicis Conseil)
Key creatives
Chief Creative Officer Bruno Bertelli; Nestlé global creative director Kike Gutierrez; ECD Dave Monk; Creative Director Polina Zabrodskaya; director Henry Mason

Why emotional storytelling rather than experiential or stunt? Nobody in the audience took part in anything. Viewers watch a staged, emotional narrative, and the brand appears only at the end as the setting for reconnection.

Psychology: scarcity, applied to people

The film's core lever is scarcity. We usually think of scarcity in terms of products, but here it's applied to relationships. Out of tens of thousands of people, only a few truly matter, which makes those few feel more precious.

It also relies on the self-referencing effect, arguably the film's strongest mechanism. Each question (do you know my nickname, have you seen me cry) quietly prompts viewers to run the same test on their own lives. You aren't just watching his crowd shrink, you're picturing yours.

There's a note of nostalgia too. The ad touches on friends we've lost touch with, then offers a simple, low-effort way back: have a coffee. It's a gentle reminder to reconnect rather than guilt-based persuasion. And through emotional transfer, the warmth of that reunion becomes associated with NESCAFÉ Gold itself.

These are explanations of why the film could resonate, not findings from published audience research.

Storytelling: a funnel you can feel

The structure is essentially a funnel. It opens with overwhelming scale, a full amphitheatre, and narrows with each question until only a handful of faces remain. That steady reduction builds tension and makes the final moment land.

The questions do the narrative work. Each one moves from surface to depth, from knowing a name to having seen someone cry, so the film charts the layers of intimacy without any exposition.

The product is held back until the end. Coffee isn't the hero of the story; it's the excuse for the reunion. That restraint lets the emotion breathe and positions NESCAFÉ Gold as the backdrop to connection rather than the subject of the ad.

The setting adds weight. An ancient Roman theatre gives the scene a sense of timelessness and scale, suggesting that the need for close bonds is as old as humanity.

Positioning: premium instant coffee, premium moments

Instant coffee has a perception problem. It's often seen as the functional, cheaper option next to café coffee. The relaunch tackled that on two fronts: an upgraded product and an emotional repositioning. In the UK, Nestlé backed what trade press called the brand's biggest rebrand yet with a reported £7 million investment, covering a reformulated blend (Arabica beans ground ten times finer), a taller jar with a champagne gold lid across 27 products, and supporting marketing. Nestlé also cited research that NESCAFÉ Gold shoppers spend 47% more per trip than the average instant coffee shopper.

The line "For the Moments That Matter" moves NESCAFÉ Gold from a caffeine fix to the coffee you share with people who matter. Nestlé framed the relaunch as a way to compete in the fast-growing premium coffee segment, and attaching the product to rare, meaningful moments is a way of justifying a premium choice.

The campaign linked the care people put into relationships with the care that goes into making a cup of NESCAFÉ Gold. That's the bridge between the emotional film and the product story.

Cultural relevance: connection in the age of social media

The film arrived at a moment of growing public conversation about whether social media had left people with many connections but few close friends. Commentators at the time read the ad as a gentle critique of collecting online "friends" instead of nurturing real ones.

NESCAFÉ had explored this territory before. A few years earlier, NESCAFÉ France and Publicis Conseil ran "Really Friends?", a genuine real-world experiment in which a Facebook user tried to meet his online friends over coffee. "Those Few People" takes that idea into a scripted, cinematic form.

The theme is also universal. Losing touch with people you once cared about is a near-universal experience, which helped the film travel across European markets and later to Australia and New Zealand.

Execution: one hero film, many markets

The centrepiece was the 90-second "Those Few People" film, launched first in the UK on TV, in cinemas and online. Shorter product-focused spots carried the relaunch message about the new recipe and packaging.

The campaign was built to travel. After the UK, it rolled out across Europe as part of NESCAFÉ Gold's regional relaunch. In 2018, the emotional platform was extended to Australia and New Zealand through a locally developed campaign, "When Taste Matters." Publicis Groupe's Sydney team (credited as Marcel Sydney in some reports) built on the original idea with two new TV commercials, influencer content, outdoor advertising reaching commuters, and NESCAFÉ Gold pop-up cafés with in-store baristas.

Results

YouTube reach
According to the campaign's Cannes Lions submission, the film reached 53 million people across markets on YouTube (the entry doesn't specify unique viewers or a measurement period)
UK relaunch investment
Reported £7 million, covering product, packaging and marketing

No published sales or brand-tracking figures tie the film to relaunch performance, so its impact is best described through reach rather than proven business results.

A fair critique

The biggest risk with emotional storytelling is a weak link to the product. The film is moving, but the coffee could be swapped for almost any shared drink. Some reader comments on Australian trade site Mumbrella made exactly this point, questioning what the story had to do with coffee (comments are anecdotal, not representative research).

The 80,000 figure also deserves care. It's the brand's estimate, used as a creative premise, and the campaign materials don't cite a scientific source for it.

And the "social experiment" framing that often follows this ad online is inaccurate. It's a staged film. For an archive analysis, it's worth separating this kind of experiment-as-story from genuine experiments like NESCAFÉ's own "Really Friends?".

Why it works: the mechanism

  • Scarcity applied to relationships. Turning a big number into a tiny remaining group makes close bonds feel precious.
  • Self-referencing. Each question pulls viewers into running the test on their own lives.
  • Emotional transfer. Coffee appears as the reason to reconnect, so the warmth of the reunion attaches to the brand without the product interrupting the story.
  • Emotion supporting premiumisation. NESCAFÉ wasn't only saying its coffee tasted better. It was making the coffee feel worthy of life's most meaningful moments, which supports a premium relaunch.

The takeaway

"Those Few People" shows how a brand can turn a statistic into a feeling. It doesn't sell coffee; it sells the moment the coffee makes room for, which is exactly the job a premium relaunch needed the brand to do.
